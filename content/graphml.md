# Graph-based learning and design of architected solids

Architected solids, or mechanical metamaterials, obtain their properties from the topology of a repeating lattice rather than from the chemistry of the base material. That makes them an unusually good target for machine learning: the design space is combinatorial and large, the physics is well understood at the strut level, and the quantity of interest is a property of the *network*, not of any single member. This project combines graph-based learning with in-situ measurement to understand, and eventually design, the response of architected solids.

## Lattices are graphs

A strut-based lattice is naturally a graph: joints are nodes, struts are edges, and the geometric information (node coordinates, strut cross-sections, orientation) lives as features on them. Representing a unit cell this way has two advantages over a voxel or image representation. The representation is exact for the structure, and the learning model can share weights across lattices with different numbers of struts, so that a network trained on one family of topologies can be queried on another. Graph neural networks operate on this representation by message passing: each node aggregates information from its neighbours over several rounds, which mirrors how load is redistributed through a lattice.

The targets we are interested in are the effective elastic and inelastic properties, the strength, and in particular the way the response changes with specimen size.

## Indentation size effect of architected solids

A central part of the project is the `indentation size effect (ISE)` of architected solids: the apparent hardness and stiffness measured by an indenter depend on the ratio between the indenter size and the lattice cell size, in a way that differs from the size effect seen in homogeneous crystalline solids. We studied this through combined theoretical and computational analyses of several types of architected solids, covering stretching- and bending-dominated topologies.

The computational work is anchored by in-situ experiments:

- **In-situ X-ray computed tomography (CT)** of indentation tests, so that the deforming lattice is imaged in three dimensions during loading.
- **Digital volume correlation (DVC)** on the CT volumes to extract the full three-dimensional displacement and strain fields inside the lattice, strut by strut.

These measurements give exactly the kind of data a graph-based model needs: node displacements and edge strains for a known topology under a known load. They serve both as ground truth for learning and as the basis for the mechanistic explanation of the size effect (see *Mechanisms of size effects in elastic architected solids*, Acta Materialia, in the publication list).

## Towards inverse design

The forward model (topology to property) is the first half; the second is inverse design, where a target property is mapped back to candidate topologies. Working in the graph domain makes the inverse problem tractable, because the candidates are generated by local edits to the graph (adding, removing or reorienting struts) and can be scored quickly by the learned surrogate before any of them is printed and tested. The size-effect work is the physical constraint on that search: a designed lattice only has a well-defined property if the component is large enough relative to the cell for the property to converge.
